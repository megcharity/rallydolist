The four rules

Praise in public. Correct in private.
Tough on standards, not on people.
Constant feedback, positive and corrective.
If it’s not documented, it didn’t happen.
What a feedback culture looks like
  • Catching someone doing something great, and telling them specifically, in the moment
  • Checking in with team members mid-shift, not just at the start or end
  • Addressing small issues before they become patterns
  • Making coaching feel like a conversation, not a verdict
  • Following up after feedback: did it land? Do they need more support?
No surprises. No team member should ever be surprised by a corrective action conversation. If they are, we waited too long to give them feedback that could have helped earlier.

Praise in public. Correct in private.

Where and how you deliver feedback matters as much as what you say.

Praise in public
  • Call out great work in lineups, team channels and end-of-shift recaps
  • Be specific: “great energy on the floor tonight” lands harder than “good job”
  • Recognize effort as well as results
  • Make it a habit: frequent small recognition beats rare big moments
Correct in private
  • Always in a private space, away from the floor and other team members
  • Preferably at the end of the shift, never in the heat of the moment if it can wait
  • Have a second manager as a witness for any formal conversation
  • Stay calm, specific and focused on behavior, not personality
Never call someone out in front of their peers or guests, whatever the situation. The goal of corrective feedback is always to help the person succeed, not to punish them.

The five steps

How we approach every coaching conversation, from a quick check-in to a formal corrective session.

  1. PrepareGather the facts first: attendance records, schedules, policies, witness statements. Have specific examples, decide what success looks like, and prepare the documentation in advance. Choose a private space and arrange a witness.
  2. Be specific, not generalNot “you seemed checked out tonight” but “you weren't acknowledging guests at the host desk between 7pm and 8pm.”
  3. Make it a conversationGive them space to respond and share context. Ask questions before making assumptions. You're solving a problem together.
  4. Agree on a path forwardWhat needs to change, by when, and what support they need. Confirm expectations, next steps, and the consequences if improvement isn't achieved.
  5. Document and follow upNote it in the shift report, complete the form where applicable, and check in over the following shifts.

Who can do what

Coaching conversations and verbal warnings can generally be handled by the manager. Anything more serious needs HR or senior leadership before you meet with the employee.

LevelWhat it isBefore you meet
Coaching conversationAddressing a concern early with clear expectationsManager can handle
Verbal warningA documented conversation; the team member is made aware of the issue and given a clear path to improvementManager can handle
Written warningThe issue continued or escalated; consequences of continued noncompliance are clearly statedHR or senior leadership must review and approve
Final warningThe last step before terminationHR or senior leadership must review and approve
Suspension or termination recommendation—HR or senior leadership must review and approve
Not sure which level fits? Consider how serious the issue is, the employee's prior history and the circumstances, and consult HR before proceeding. Thinking about skipping a step? Consult HR first.
Needs immediate action, or the employee shouldn't stay at work? Contact HR or senior leadership right away. If they're unavailable and it's in the best interest of the business, you may send the employee home pending review. Tell them they are not being terminated, but shouldn't return until HR or senior leadership directs them to. Don't make promises or speculate about the outcome.

Writing it up

Pick the warning level and the warning type (attendance, tardiness, performance, conduct, policy violation, safety, insubordination or other). Name the policy, or write “N/A” if it's a performance or coaching issue.

Description of the issue

What happened + when + the expectation not met + the impact

“John has a bad attitude.”

“On July 10, John interrupted a guest while they were speaking and walked away before resolving the concern.”

Expected improvement

What must change + when + how success is measured

“Arrive ready to work at your scheduled start time for every shift. If you can't report as scheduled, notify your manager as soon as possible per company policy. Immediate and sustained improvement is expected.”

Instead of…Write…
“Needs a better attitude”The specific behavior that needs to change
“Always late”The specific dates and times
“Needs to improve”Exactly what successful improvement looks like
Observable facts only. No opinions, assumptions, labels or emotionally charged language. Let the employee respond, and record any relevant comments on the form.

Witnesses & paperwork

Before the conversation
  • Know the objective: performance or conduct?
  • Quiet, private space with few interruptions
  • A second manager present as a witness. Best practice: the witness is the opposite sex of the employee
  • Be ready with specifics and a timeline for change
  • Come ready to listen
  • Have the form ready to complete during or right after
After the conversation
  • Both parties sign the form
  • Save it to the employee's file and submit it to HR for their personnel file
  • Note the conversation in the shift report: factual and brief
  • Sensitive details go to HR, never into the shift report
  • Follow up in the following shifts to monitor progress and offer support

Open door & workplace conduct

Any team member can bring a work-related or personal concern to anyone on the leadership team, at any time, without fear of retaliation.

If a team member comes to you
  • Listen fully and thank them for trusting you
  • Document the conversation
  • Address it, or escalate immediately if it's beyond your scope
  • Handle it with care, urgency and discretion
Report to HR immediately
  • Harassment
  • Discrimination
  • Retaliation
  • Workplace violence or threats
  • Safety concerns and other policy violations
Retaliation of any kind is strictly prohibited. Talking about a guest or team member in a demeaning way isn't tolerated anywhere: on the floor, in the back of house, or elsewhere. Conduct that creates a hostile environment is addressed immediately and escalated to leadership and HR.
HR contact: HR@rallypickleball.com. Unsure how to handle an employee relations issue? Contact HR before you act.
