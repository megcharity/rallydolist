Who we are

Rally is a lifestyle brand that celebrates an active social life. We exist to be a catalyst for connection, transforming how active people socialize and social people move.

Founded in 2021 by Barrett Worthington and Meg Charity, Rally grew out of Picklefest, a four-day pickleball and dining gathering in Charlottesville, Virginia. The goal is to be the leading venue-based pickleball entertainment platform in the world.

The food is elevated but approachable: global street food and Neapolitan-style pizza from our Marra Forni brick oven, built to share and eat on the go. The bar, designed by an award-winning mixologist, offers house originals, classics and spirit-free drinks that stand on their own.

Our voice: confident, warm and refined, with a quiet wit and a touch of irreverence. Playful and conversational, never cheesy, punny, stuffy or trying too hard.

How we deliver

  • Professionalism: you are “on stage” from the moment you arrive until the last guest leaves. No gossip about the business or teammates where guests can hear.
  • Positive attitude: a sincere smile sets the tone. Never let a tip change your attitude.
  • Aim to please: look for ways to say “yes” and honor requests immediately.
  • Details: a guest’s visit is hundreds of details; the little things are often what they remember.
  • Teamwork and speed: every guest is your responsibility. Bias toward speed while mirroring the guest’s pace, never rushed and never slow.
  • Constant improvement: everything we do, we can do better.

Look the part

Guests judge you mostly before you speak: presentation is 60% visual and grooming, 30% tone, 10% words.

Grooming standards

  • Uniform: clean, ironed, well fitted, to your position’s style guide. Clean shoes with slip-resistant soles.
  • Hair: off the face; shoulder length or longer is tied up with black or neutral ties. Facial hair neatly trimmed; clean-shaven at the neckline.
  • No cologne or perfume, since it competes with the food and bothers sensitive guests.
  • Jewelry minimal: no long dangly earrings or stacks of rings, necklaces or bracelets.
  • Nails clean; polish is fine only if it isn’t chipped.

Body language

Pause, come set, engage. Stand tall without leaning on chairs, tables or ledges. Pivot to face the guest, nod, smile while you speak (they can hear it), and listen with intent. No pen clicking or tapping. Walk with purpose. Never touch your hair or face in view of guests.

Part 2 · Serving guests

Six points of service

Hospitality, presentation, knowledge, teamwork, critical thinking and technical proficiency. The aim: take care of every need before the guest expresses it.

1. Hospitality

  • The Two-Foot Rule: acknowledge every guest within two feet of you with eye contact, energy and a smile. Mid-task? Pause, smile and say “I’ll be right with you!”
  • Never the C-word: they are guests, not customers. They chose to spend their time with us.
  • Make Magic Moments: greet regulars by name, have a regular’s usual coffee ready before they order, bring more warm pita when a Dip It Board runs low without being asked.
  • Little things make big things: open doors, give right of way, walk guests to the restroom or court, thank teammates by name.

Rally borrows Danny Meyer’s idea that great hospitality is 51% feeling and 49% task. The five emotional skills behind it: optimistic warmth, intelligence (curiosity), work ethic, empathy, and self-awareness with integrity. Our service should feel unexpected, undeserved and unnecessary.

2. Presentation

Covered in Look the part. The first ten words you say create a lasting impression, so open with something like “Hello, welcome to Rally. Is this your first time joining us?” or “Welcome back, great to see you again!”

3. Knowledge

Know the menu, how dishes are prepared (so you know which special requests are possible), and our practices such as court reservations and to-go. Knowledge builds confidence, confidence builds ability, ability builds sales, and sales build income. Not being able to answer questions about pickleball, food or drinks is one of the five worst things we can do.

4. Teamwork

If food is up while you’re in the kitchen, run it. Passing the bar and a drink is waiting, deliver it. A table needs clearing, silverware or a wipe-down, do it. A guest flags you, answer or find out. A teammate needs help, give it graciously.

  • Communicate: if you can’t do something yourself, hand it off clearly so nothing slips through the cracks, and always follow through on what you’re asked.
  • Circulate: find at least one need at every table, in your section or not. Never congregate with teammates on the floor. Never let a table sit dirty and unset.
  • Consolidate: full hands in and out. Take dirties in; bring food or restock items (china, glass, silver) out.

5. Critical thinking

Read the guest; don’t treat every table the same. If a guest mentions a dislike or allergy, carry it through their whole visit. Mirror their pace: a guest at the 101 Bar who orders a round of beers but no food yet may want to linger. Think about how differently you’d serve four businesspeople at happy hour, retirees at morning open play, a young couple on a date, and a first-time birthday group.

6. Technical proficiency

Proactive, unobtrusive, “open service” that respects personal space. Details are in Technical standards. One rule overrides the rest: never sacrifice a guest’s comfort to follow a rule.

How to interact with guests

The six non-negotiables

  1. Start and finish every guest interaction with a smile.
  2. Use names when addressing guests and colleagues.
  3. Never, ever interrupt.
  4. Before leaving any table, manicure it: remove anything no longer needed.
  5. Say hello while giving guests the right of way, naturally and gracefully.
  6. Guests never hear conversations about our business or your personal life.

Also expected

  • Be confident and casual, never formal, rehearsed or rote.
  • Show confidence in our food and drinks; never qualify how we prepare them.
  • Don’t repeat a guest’s order unless you need to clarify, and don’t announce your next move (“I’ll ring in your drinks and be right back”).
  • Never use slang or draw attention to guests moving through crowded areas.
  • Escort guests partway (“let me show you the way”) and point with your whole hand, never a finger.
  • Address children and guests with disabilities as individuals, with the utmost care.
  • Never leave a guest with a “no.” Offer an alternative.
  • Let a guest offer a handshake; don’t initiate one.

Leading and listening

Don’t be an order-taker. Ask probing questions (“something lighter, or more filling?”, “a hoppy IPA or something lighter?”), then make specific, vivid recommendations. A guest in a hurry gets steered to something quick like a Caesar salad. A guest who’s waited a long time for a seat gets a snack right away. Listen to tone as well as words: irritated, content or rushed.

Do

  • Approach silently and wait for acknowledgement. If guests are deep in conversation, rest a hand on the table and wait for eye contact.
  • Fold a guest’s napkin and place it on the table when they step away.
  • Bring missing utensils before anyone asks; leave tables dry and crumb-free after clearing.

Don’t

  • Lean, crouch, kneel or ever sit at a guest’s table.
  • Let your eyes wander to other tables while talking, or show irritation.
  • Get drawn into negative conversation with guests or co-workers.
  • Ask a cash-paying guest if they need change. Say you’ll be right back with it unless they tell you otherwise, and never look at a payment on the table in view of guests.
Day-to-day standards (all 18)
  1. Be a positive force each day and give energy to your team.
  2. You’re on stage whenever you’re in uniform, including arriving and leaving.
  3. Let guests initiate handshakes.
  4. Express hospitality through a genuine smile.
  5. Never touch your face, or cough or sneeze into your hands, in view of guests; if it happens, wash immediately.
  6. Sick? Tell your manager right away and try to find coverage. A doctor’s note is mandatory.
  7. Handle glassware by the bottom third, never near the rim.
  8. Hot plates for hot food, cold plates for cold food, never a warm glass.
  9. Deliver plates low, like a plane landing, never from above like a helicopter.
  10. Practice acute listening; bring exactly what was asked for and clarify if unsure.
  11. Keep noise down: don’t toss silver, slam plates, drop bottles or slam doors. Know your steward’s name.
  12. Scan every table you pass for manicures and guests trying to catch your eye.
  13. Cultivate grace: anticipate needs without imposing yourself.
  14. Report any bug or rodent sighting to a manager immediately.
  15. Pause before joining a conversation; if there’s no opening, step away and circle back.
  16. Leftovers: bring boxes so guests can box their own; box for them at the table if asked.
  17. Close every experience with a pause of warmth and thanks.
  18. Learn names, and offer yours again to people who may have forgotten it.

Sequence of service

Every tab starts with a pre-authorized card, before the first order goes in.

Step one: pre-authorize a card

If the guest orders through the QR code, Toast asks them to pre-authorize a card before they order. If they order with you on a handheld or POS, get a card from them and pre-authorize the tab before ringing anything in. Guests can still close out on that card or pay another way at the end.

Toast Mobile Order & Pay

Every court, table and bar seat has its own QR code. Guests scan with their phone camera and order; others in the party can scan and add to the same tab. A Captain or Bartender can split checks.

Mirror the guest. If they prefer traditional service, take the order on a handheld and offer a physical menu. You can also start the order and let them add via QR. At every hand-off between teammates, pass along allergies, preferences and the occasion.

Five critical moments

Guests want service faster when they first sit down (drink), when a course is finished (manicure and crumb), when a drink is low (refill), when food arrives and something is missing (quality check), and when it’s time to go (payment).

Glassware

  • Hold by the bottom third; stemmed glasses by the stem; mugs by the bottom third or handle. This applies to empty glasses too.
  • Carrying more than two drinks? Use a tray. Never stack glasses inside each other on the floor.
  • Place on the guest’s right with your left hand (on the left for a left-handed guest). At a banquette, use your right hand for guests on your left and vice versa. Water goes farthest right.
  • Coasters come with the first round. Iced tea gets an iced teaspoon on the table to its right; iced tea and lemonade get a lemon wedge at 3 o’clock.

Coffee and tea service

  • Mugs go on the guest’s right with your right hand, handle at 3 o’clock. A refill gets a fresh cup.
  • Lattes, cappuccinos and espresso drinks are carried by the saucer, handle at 3 o’clock, spoon across the top with its handle facing out.
  • Hot tea service standards were still to be decided when the manual was written. Ask your manager.

Plates: delivering

  • Carry three plates at most, by the edge, never with your hand near food. No “butterfly” carries; use a tray or a teammate.
  • Serve from the left with the left hand, counterclockwise, at hip level, angling your body so the other plates stay out of the guest’s space.
  • Announce each dish and be ready to describe it. Then step back and check: does the table have everything it needs? Close with “please enjoy.”

Plates: clearing and stacking

  • Clear from the right with the right hand, clockwise, at hip level. Never interrupt a guest to clear.
  • Ask the guest if it’s okay to clear their empty dishes. Clear ladies’ plates first when possible.
  • Largest plate in your left hand, second on your left arm, then stack flatware, ramekins and small plates on those two; third plate in your right hand. Plates with food left go last.
  • Never stack on or over the table, and stack quietly. Can’t clear it all? Ask a teammate to follow and finish.

Manicuring and crumbing

  • Manicure at natural breaks: remove everything tied to a finished course. Be purposeful, not a grab-and-go.
  • Crumb after plates are cleared, sweeping crumbs with a rolled towel onto a small plate, never the floor.
  • Consolidate visits: bring water refills when you manicure, check other drinks, and bring a teammate to clear a big table in one go.

Part 3 · Safety and rules

Responsible alcohol service

It is against the law to serve a minor or an intoxicated person, and you can be held personally responsible.

Know North Carolina’s rules on responsible vendors, dram shop liability and BAC levels. Card anyone who appears under 30; no valid photo ID proving 21+, no alcohol. Decline politely, without judgment, and firmly.

Watch for signs of intoxication

Slurred speech, glassy or unfocused eyes, strong smell of alcohol, blank stare, impaired judgment (buying a round for everyone), annoying other guests, profanity or bragging, flushed or pale skin, stumbling or spilling, sudden changes in volume or talkativeness, flashes of anger, exaggerated emotion or responses, and trouble answering coherently.

Be careful: a loud personality isn’t intoxication; tolerance hides signs without lowering impairment; and illnesses such as stroke or diabetes can look like intoxication. Track how much each guest has had, and remember they may have been drinking elsewhere.

What raises intoxication: drinking faster (the liver clears about ½ oz of alcohol per hour), lower body weight, no food before or during drinking, fatigue, and poor health or medications.

Cutting a guest off

  1. Stop serving immediately and tell the manager on duty (MOD). Tell your teammates so the guest can’t order elsewhere.
  2. If they order again, acknowledge it and have the MOD visit them. The MOD will back you up, whoever the guest is.
  3. Approach privately and kindly: say you’re concerned for their safety, and that you’re happy to serve food and non-alcoholic drinks but no more alcohol.
  4. If asked why, restate your concern. Don’t argue or try to reason; bring in the MOD.
  5. Never physically detain a guest. If they’re belligerent, approach with a teammate; call the police if it continues.
  6. Strongly discourage driving and arrange an Uber or Lyft. Let them know they’re welcome to stay.

Guest complaints: the four A’s

Whether the guest is right doesn’t matter. They believe we wronged them, and the goal is to fix it.

  1. Apologize: say sorry for how it affected them, even if it wasn’t our fault. No excuses, no blame.
  2. Acknowledge: show you understand. If the guest feels there’s a problem, there is one.
  3. Act: tell a manager, fix it quickly, and loop in anyone who needs to know.
  4. Assess: go back to the guest. Do they feel taken care of now?

Notify the MOD immediately about every guest issue, big or small.

Exception: food poisoning or illness claims

Never apologize for the cause. Say only: “I’m sorry to hear you weren’t feeling well. Let me have you speak with our General Manager.” Never say anything like “I’m sorry you got sick from eating at Rally,” which can be read as accepting fault.

Allergies and diets

An allergic reaction can be fatal. Captains and Bartenders always ask whether the kitchen should know about any allergies.

  1. Reassure the guest and confirm their choices are free of the allergen. Our menu is shared, so talk through every item for the table that might contain it. Overcommunicate.
  2. Always involve a manager and double-check recipes with them.
  3. Ring items in with the correct allergy modifiers in Toast.
  4. Make sure the expeditor knows when the food is rung in.

90% of food allergies come from five sources: peanuts, tree nuts, milk and eggs, seafood and shellfish, and soy and wheat. Reactions include wheezing, hoarseness, shortness of breath, trouble swallowing, swelling of the face, lips, hands or feet, hives, itching, and loss of consciousness.

Hidden sources of common allergens
  • Wheat: breads, pita, pizza, breaded foods, pasta, crackers, waffles, most cookies, flour, semolina, durum, bran. Less obvious: barley malt, chocolate, cocoa, MSG, soy sauce, tamari, some alcoholic drinks.
  • Gluten: like wheat-free, plus rye, oats and barley. Gluten triggers celiac disease and hides under other names on labels.
  • Dairy: milk, cream, butter, cheese, whey, casein, lactose. Often in cakes, breads, batters, sauces, gravies, soups, dressings and some sausages.
  • Egg: mayonnaise, aioli, tartar sauce, meatballs, meatloaf, frosting, cream pies, macaroons, ice cream and many baked goods.
  • Corn: corn syrup, oil and starch, cornmeal, and many vinegars, pickles, dressings, mustard, mayo, relish; possibly cheese, vanilla, xanthan gum, MSG and gelatin.
  • Yeast-free diets avoid fermented items: vinegar, pickles, aged cheese, soy sauce, Worcestershire, alcohol, malt, processed meats. Lemon juice can replace vinegar; fresh cheese can replace aged.

Health code

Some rules are obvious; others aren’t, like an empty paper towel dispenser counting as a violation.

  • Wash your hands after touching your hair or face, and every time you enter a restroom, even if you didn’t use it.
  • Anything that holds food (ice buckets, clean glass racks) never touches the ground.
  • Ice scoops live in their holster, never in the ice. Never scoop ice with a glass. Ice wells hold nothing but ice.
  • You may place a garnish on a drink rim with a clean bare hand.
  • Hand sinks are only for handwashing, never for dumping food or drinks, and must always have paper towels in the dispenser. Every toilet must have paper.
  • No partially eaten food in any BOH area: kitchen, bars, Café, behind the front desk, storage or wait stations. Staff drinks there must be in an unbreakable cup with a lid and straw.
  • Personal items (bags, shoes, clothing) go in designated areas, never near food or beverage storage.
  • Any fly or fruit fly in the building is a violation.
  • Perishables held above 41°F are discarded after 4 hours. Everything prepped is labeled with day, date, time, item, use-by date and your initials.
  • Rotate everything first in, first out (FIFO); keep products covered and store-fronted with labels facing out.

Workplace safety

  • Spills: whoever causes one cleans it up immediately; stand by it to warn others until it’s clean.
  • No running. Give right of way, and call out when you’re behind someone or coming around a blind corner.
  • Knives stay where they’re used. Carry them tip down, and let a falling knife fall.
  • Broken glass: never pick it up by hand. Sweep it into a dustpan and use the glass-only bin.
  • Electrical: don’t use frayed or broken cords or plugs; report them. Never stand in water around electrical equipment.
  • Use only equipment you’re trained on, unplug machines before cleaning, and keep guards in place.
  • Assume every pot, pan, plate and surface is hot; use dry towels or mitts and warn the dish team. Direct steam away from you.
  • Small grill fires: baking soda or a small extinguisher. Don’t take risks with your safety.
  • Never lift more than you safely can, and never more than 50 lb. Use a hand truck or dolly.

Coffee basics

Coffee comes from the seeds of the coffee cherry, mostly hand-picked because cherries on one branch ripen at different times. It grows between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn; specialty coffee usually at 2,500–6,500 ft. Brewing water should be just off the boil, 195–205°F.

  • Arabica vs. Robusta: Arabica is about 70% of the world’s coffee and far better in the cup. Robusta is cheaper, blander and higher in caffeine, so it ends up in instant coffee and cheap blends.
  • Cold brew: grounds steep in cold water for 10–20 hours, then get strained into a concentrate for iced coffee.
  • Crema: the caramel-colored foam on top of a well-pulled espresso.
  • Roast: darker roasts mute a coffee’s origin flavors and bring bittersweet notes forward.

Tea basics

All true tea comes from the same plant; the type depends on processing. Herbal “teas” come from other plants and aren’t technically tea.

  • White: unopened buds, simply picked, steamed and dried. Light, fresh, bittersweet finish.
  • Green: steamed or wok-fired to stop oxidation. Light green, sweet and savory.
  • Oolong: partly oxidized; ranges from floral to sweet and roasted.
  • Black: fully oxidized; the most common tea, flavor depends on origin.
  • Pu-erh: aged and fermented for years; rich, earthy, full-bodied.

Spirits and cocktails

Every spirit is made in two steps. Fermentation creates alcohol (yeast + sugar = alcohol + CO₂), and distillation separates and concentrates it with heat.

Tequila ages and cognac grades

Tequila

  • Blanco / silver: unaged or up to 60 days in steel; the purest agave flavor.
  • Joven / gold: usually unaged mixto, colored and flavored with additives.
  • Reposado: “rested” in wood 2 months to a year, often 3–9 months.
  • Añejo: aged at least 1 year, best at 18 months to 3 years.
  • Extra añejo: over 4 years; a sipping tequila.

Cognac

The age on the label is the youngest brandy in the blend. V.S. (three stars) is under 4½ years; V.S.O.P. 4½–6½ years; X.O. and Napoleon 5½ years and up, as old as 40.

Scotch regions

“Single malt” means one distillery, not one barrel. Speyside: elegant, honey and pear. Highlands: robust caramel, malt and spice. Lowlands: delicate, floral and fruity. Islay: intensely smoky and peaty. Islands: iodine and smoke with fruit. Campbeltown: Springbank, from lightly smoky to heavily peated.

How we taste spirits
  1. Look: color and richness.
  2. Aroma: nose with your mouth open and build a list of descriptors.
  3. Taste: always spit when tasting at work. Use uniform glassware; note the beginning, middle and end; matching aroma and taste is a marker of quality. On the finish, consider acidity, burn, length and how the flavor evolves.
  4. Evaluate: category, quality, brand, and whether you enjoy it.

Beer

Four ingredients: malted barley (lightly kilned for caramel notes, dark-roasted for chocolate or coffee), hops (bitterness and aromas like citrus, pine or grass), yeast (turns sugar into alcohol and adds flavor) and water (90–95% of beer).

Pairing beer with food: complement similar flavors (coriander-spiced dish with a Belgian witbier), contrast opposites like sweet and sour, or cut big, rich or spicy food with a beer that tames it.

Styles guests ask about

  • IPA (India Pale Ale): very hoppy, coppery and fruity. Named for the extra hops used to preserve ale on the long sea voyage from England to India.
  • American pale ale: bitter hoppiness with fruit; a gentler step toward an IPA.
  • Porter vs. stout: both dark and roasty. Porters are drier and lighter in flavor; dry stouts get their coffee character from roasted barley.
  • Milk and oatmeal stouts: milk stouts are sweetened with milk sugar and lower in alcohol; oatmeal stouts are silky and smooth.
  • Imperial stout: big, strong and fruity, first brewed for export to the Russian imperial court.
  • Pilsner: first brewed in 1842 in Pilsen, Czech Republic. The palest lager and the most brewed style in the world.
  • Märzen: amber, toasty lager traditionally brewed in March and stored until the fall festivals.
  • Lambic and Trappist ales: lambics are dry Belgian wheat beers with fruit; Trappist ales are strong dark ales brewed by monks.

Water shaped beer history: soft Czech water makes Pilsner taste clean, while hard English water gives British pale ales their bite.

Part 5 · Pickleball

Rules of the game

Every team member should be able to explain the basics. Pickleball mixes tennis, badminton and ping-pong, and is played singles or doubles on a badminton-sized court with a paddle and a plastic ball with holes.

It was invented in 1965 on Bainbridge Island, Washington, by Joel Pritchard and Bill Bell (with Barney McCallum soon after) as a game the whole family could play. It’s America’s fastest-growing sport.

The four rules guests ask about

  • Serve: from behind the baseline, underhand with an upward swing, contact below the waist, diagonally into the opponent’s court past the kitchen line. One attempt only. Bouncing the ball first is allowed.
  • Double-bounce rule: the serve must bounce before the return, and the return must bounce before the third shot. After that, volleys are allowed. It’s the most commonly broken rule.
  • The kitchen (No Volley Zone): the 7-foot zone on each side of the net, line included. You can stand in it and hit balls that bounce, but you can’t volley while touching it, including momentum carrying you in after the shot.
  • Lines are in: a ball that touches a line counts as in.

Court etiquette

  • Never interrupt a rally. Wait until it ends before speaking to players; distracting them is unsafe and frustrating.
  • “Let!” Anyone calls it to stop play for a safety issue or distraction; that rally is replayed.
  • Ball onto your court: stop, call “Let,” return the ball, replay the rally.
  • Your ball onto another court: if they’re playing, call “Ball!” and stay on your court; don’t chase it. If the court is empty, you can retrieve it.
Key pickleball terms
  • Dink: a soft drop hit from the kitchen line that lands low near the opponent’s feet.
  • Drive / drop / lob: a hard shot / a soft shot landing at the opponent’s feet / a shot over the opponent’s head.
  • Third-shot drop: the serving team’s soft third shot to get to the net.
  • Volley / groundstroke: hit before the bounce / after the bounce.
  • Even / odd side: right / left half of the court.
  • Transition: the area between baseline and kitchen line.
  • Reset / speed-up / hand battle: neutralizing a hard shot / suddenly hitting hard / a fast volley exchange.
  • Banger: a player who only drives.
  • Spin serve: spinning the ball on the toss, which is illegal.

Helping guests choose a paddle

There’s no single best paddle; it’s the one that gives the player the most confidence. Encourage demos. A 16 mm core is softer with less power; 14 mm is stiffer with more power. A 5.5″ handle suits two-handed backhands. Heavier paddles give stability and power; lighter ones give quick hands and touch.
